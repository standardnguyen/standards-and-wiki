# N agreeing sources are not N sources

## The shape

You are researching something that matters, and you want corroboration. You find it: four pages, three domains, all stating the same fact in different words. They do not link to each other. They read as independent.

You record the fact with confidence proportional to the count. Four sources agreed.

They did not agree. One wrote it, and the other three copied. **Agreement among copies of one origin is a measurement of how widely a claim propagated, and you read it as a measurement of how likely it is to be true.** Those are unrelated quantities, and the second one is the one you needed.

## Why it survives review

Because the pages genuinely look independent — different domains, different layouts, different wording, no visible citation between them. Nothing in a rendered page tells you where its sentence came from.

And the corroboration instinct is correct in general. Independent observers agreeing *is* strong evidence. The failure is not the instinct; it is that you have no way to test independence by reading the outputs. **Redundancy tests the readers, not the claim.** Four readers agreeing tells you the claim is persuasive. Nothing more.

There is also a version where the copies are visible and you still count them: a fact stated in the summary and the body of the same page, or repeated in three sections of one document. Same upstream, and being *inside* one artifact does not make it two.

## The general form

**Counting statements is not measuring evidence. Ask what the upstream is before you count anything.**

The tell is homogeneity. Independent observers disagree in detail — they round differently, hedge differently, emphasize differently. **Copies are *identical* where an independent confirmation would have varied.** When four sources say the same number, in the same precision, with the same framing and no disagreement anywhere in the detail, you are looking at one sentence wearing four coats.

When the claim is a contested one, add a second reading: **two right answers to different questions.** A genuine disagreement between a canonical source and a copy sometimes means they are answering different questions — a global versus a regional figure, a default versus a configured value. Check that before calling either one wrong.

## The sharpest form — it applies to your own agents

The mechanism is at its worst when the redundant observers are *your own*, because then the agreement arrives pre-blessed as independence.

Two subagents, on different lanes, different tasks, different prompts, each independently file the same finding. A reconciliation register records it as the cycle's strongest signal. The finding is false.

**The shared upstream was the document they were both handed.** Both read the same briefing line, both reasoned correctly from it, both arrived at the same sound inference from a false premise. Two processes, one input. Everything about the setup read as independence — separate runs, separate contexts, separate lanes — and the independence was notional.

The asymmetry is what makes it expensive: **you cannot see agreement as non-evidence when you are the one who fanned out the work.** Parallelism *feels* like triangulation. If the fan-out shares an input, it is a single observation reported N times.

Special case worth naming: when the shared upstream is a *stale status line*, the inference is not merely wrong, it is confidently wrong in the direction of alarm. Two agents agreeing that a hole is open is the claim nobody wants to be first to dismiss, so it accumulates confidence on restatement instead of being tested. The test was one command.

## What it costs

A false finding that has been escalated an authority tier by the counting, and which is now harder to kill than a single wrong claim — because killing it means contradicting a corroborated result. The register's confidence label is itself the obstacle.

Downstream, the copy gets *made*. A fact confirmed by four sources gets written into a fifth, which cites the four. The count grows and the evidence stays at one.

## The defense

**Probe the world. Do not count the readers.**

When a claim matters, the question is not "how many places say this" but "what is the primary source, and have I read it." One command, run against the thing itself, beats any amount of agreement among descriptions of it.

For your own agents, the defense is upstream: **hand every lane a distinct input, or treat their agreement as one report.** If a fan-out shares context — a briefing, a summary, a status line, the instructions you wrote — then the lanes are not independent observers regardless of how they are scheduled, and their consensus must be quantified as N=1. Where a fleet is the point, make the *first* wave cheap and adversarial: the useful signal is disagreement, and agreement across lanes that shared an input is not a signal at all.

## The trigger

**You are about to cite a fact with a count attached** — "confirmed by two agents," "three sources agree," "verified in four places."

And specifically: **you spawned the parallel work yourself.** The moment you generate the multiplicity is the moment you are least able to see that it is not multiplicity. Ask what they all read before you ask what they all concluded.
