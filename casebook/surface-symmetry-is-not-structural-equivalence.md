# Surface symmetry is not structural equivalence

## The shape

Two constructs sit side by side. Both have two tiers. Both split work between a working layer and a durable layer. You look at them, and the sentence forms:

> These are the same architecture.

The sentence is not false. They *do* share a shape. But the shared shape is the diagram, and the diagram is the cheapest thing to compare.

Map the parts instead:

| | construct A | construct B |
|---|---|---|
| what the tiers split on | recency — working vs. long-term | authority — canonical vs. historical |
| what it does with duplicates | collapses them semantically | keeps them, reconciles them |
| what it does with history | erases it by design | preserves it as the record |

One tier maps loosely across. The shared axiom is narrow. Everything you would have used the word "same" to justify is different.

## Why it survives review

Because a count is checkable, and the check passes. "Both have two tiers" is true, verifiable, and sits right there in the artifact. There is no step in the reasoning that a reviewer would flag, because the reasoning is *correct about the surface* and stops.

And the conclusion is pleasant. Two independent efforts converging is a satisfying story, and the satisfaction arrives before the rigor does. That ordering is the tell, not a coincidence: **the pull toward a tidy convergence is the cue to run the divergence check, not to skip it.** The more a claim flatters a clean narrative, the harder it should be to parse.

## The general form

**A shared count, depth, name, or output shape is not a structural match.** Before calling two things equivalent, name the axis each one splits on, and name what each does at the boundary. If the axes differ, the resemblance lives in the diagram and nowhere else.

The sharpest form fires in the **correcting** direction:

> **A correction inherits the confidence of the thing it corrects, and a refutation arrives sounding like a conclusion.**

So the moment you are writing a correction is the moment your own confidence is least earned — you are not verifying, you are replacing. Two shapes this takes:

- **A resemblance read as a confirmation.** A new statement sounds like it matches the record, and you revise your estimate on the resemblance instead of checking it. The revision is real, published, and driven by nothing.
- **A resemblance read as a falsification.** A claim looks contradicted by a nearby fact. A coalition of dozens of parties on an appellate docket looks like it refutes a record saying "three named plaintiffs" — then the opinion shows judgment for exactly those three, the rest dismissed for standing. The claim was right; the refutation was two false positives inferred from adjacency.

## What it costs

A wrong "fix" is worse than the stale claim it replaces, because the stale claim was already suspect and the fix arrives pre-trusted.

- A near-miss "correction" of a claim that was right, held just short of the record by one read of the source.
- A false positive escalated as a finding, because two separate passes inferred it from proximity rather than reading it.
- Two correct answers to two different questions read as a contradiction — a regional price list against a global one, an entitlement for one product tier against an entitlement for a related one — and then resolved by declaring one of them wrong.

And the residue: a closed question. "We already examined this" is a page that invites the next reader to stop reading, which makes the collapse *easier*, not harder, the second time.

## The defense

**Map the parts, then state the divergence, not just the resemblance.**

For any "these are the same" claim, answer three questions in writing:

1. What does each one split on — the same axis, or a different one that happens to produce the same number of parts?
2. What does each do at the boundary — with duplicates, with history, with failure?
3. What is the smallest case where they behave differently?

If you cannot answer 3, you do not have an equivalence, you have a resemblance.

In the correcting direction, the discipline is simpler and stricter: **verify before you revise.** A resemblance is not a check, and a correction is a claim like any other — it needs its own evidence, not the confidence of the thing it is correcting. Where the two clash, believe the source you actually read, not the shape that seemed to fit.

## The trigger

The feeling of a satisfying convergence — *"they both built the same thing,"* *"we already looked at this."*

And the specific situation where your analysis is already written down somewhere. A prior write-up marked resolved is the strongest available argument for skipping the divergence check, and it is exactly backwards: the prior analysis was of a *different request*, and having it on hand lowers the cost of collapsing them. Two identical-looking requests with divergent objectives are not one request.

Finally: any moment you catch yourself revising a number because something nearby looked like it agreed.
